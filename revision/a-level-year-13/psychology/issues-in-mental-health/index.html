This topic looks at how society has understood and treated mental illness, from the asylums to modern community care.

We will cover the history of mental health care, ways of defining abnormality, and criticisms of the medical model.

Then we look at stigma, cultural and gender differences, modern laws and policies, and how mental health problems can be prevented and managed.

Mental health in historical context: asylums, deinstitutionalisation and care in the community

Key points

  • Early asylums such as Bethlem Royal Hospital (London, founded 1247, source of the word 'bedlam') were custodial. They used restraint, isolation and sometimes public viewing, with little treatment.
  • The Lunacy Act 1845 and County Asylums Act made it compulsory for each county to build an asylum. The 1808 Act had only permitted it. Victorian asylums became huge, isolated, overcrowded institutions.
  • Moral treatment, as at Tuke's York Retreat (1796), promoted kindness, work and no chains. It was a humane reform, but later asylums drifted back to containment.
  • Early 20th-century physical treatments included insulin coma therapy, lobotomy (Moniz, 1935) and ECT (Cerletti and Bini, 1938). These were often used without informed consent.
  • Goffman (1961) described asylums as total institutions that control every aspect of life and strip away identity. Barton (1959) called the resulting passivity institutional neurosis, now called institutionalisation.
  • The Mental Treatment Act 1930 allowed voluntary admission and outpatient clinics. The Mental Health Act 1959 ended most legal certification, allowed informal admission and promoted community care.
  • Chlorpromazine (1950s, the first antipsychotic) controlled symptoms such as hallucinations. Along with Powell's 1961 'water tower' speech and the 1962 Hospital Plan, it enabled deinstitutionalisation, the closure of large psychiatric hospitals and discharge of patients.
  • Other drivers of deinstitutionalisation were scandals (e.g. Ely Hospital inquiry, 1969), Goffman's critique, human-rights concerns and cost savings. Costs are a political as well as a humanitarian motive.
  • Care in the community: the NHS and Community Care Act 1990 made local authorities assess needs and arrange care packages. The Care Programme Approach (1991) added a key worker and care plan, delivered through CPNs, day centres, group homes and crisis teams.
  • The Mental Health Act 1983 (amended 2007) governs compulsory admission, or 'sectioning'. Section 2 allows up to 28 days for assessment and Section 3 up to 6 months for treatment, renewable. Community Treatment Orders (2007) allow recall to hospital if conditions are breached.
  • Strengths of community care: it is less restrictive and reduces institutionalisation. The TAPS study (Leff et al.) followed long-stay patients from Friern and Claybury hospitals. It found most coped well, with wider social networks and no overall worsening of symptoms.
  • Weaknesses: under-funding, the revolving door of repeated readmission, homelessness, and trans-institutionalisation into prisons. Public fear after high-profile cases (e.g. Christopher Clunis, 1992) and carer burden are also problems.

In more detail

  • Timeline to learn: 1247 Bethlem founded → 1796 York Retreat → 1845 Lunacy Act → 1930 Mental Treatment Act → 1950s chlorpromazine → 1961 Enoch Powell's 'water tower' speech → 1990 NHS and Community Care Act.
  • Peak numbers: the number of patients in psychiatric hospitals in England and Wales peaked at around 150,000 in the mid-1950s, then fell sharply as hospitals closed.
  • Rosenhan's influence: Rosenhan's (1973) finding that hospital staff failed to spot sane pseudo-patients added to criticism of large psychiatric institutions.
  • Exam tip: explain deinstitutionalisation with three drivers: new drugs (chlorpromazine), critiques of institutions (Goffman, scandals) and changes in cost and policy.

Practice

Mental health in historical context: asylums, deinstitutionalisation and care in the community: 10 questions

Pick an answer to check it, or open the answer for the explanation.

1. Which 19th-century Act made it compulsory for each county to build an asylum?

  1. Lunacy Act 1845
  2. NHS and Community Care Act 1990
  3. Mental Treatment Act 1930
  4. Mental Health Act 1959
Show answer

A: Lunacy Act 1845. The 1845 Lunacy Act (with the County Asylums Act) required counties to build asylums; the 1808 Act had only permitted it. The other Acts are 20th-century and moved away from asylum-based care.

2. Enoch Powell's 1961 'water tower' speech signalled that the government planned to:

  1. close the large Victorian asylums
  2. introduce compulsory ECT for all patients
  3. build more asylums in every county
  4. abolish compulsory detention
Show answer

A: close the large Victorian asylums. Powell predicted the decline of the asylums, symbolised by their water towers. This supported the 1962 Hospital Plan and the shift to community care.

3. A person is detained under Section 2 of the Mental Health Act 1983. For how long can they initially be held for assessment?

  1. 72 hours
  2. Up to 12 months
  3. Up to 28 days
  4. Up to 6 months
Show answer

C: Up to 28 days. Section 2 allows detention for assessment for up to 28 days. Section 3 (treatment) lasts up to 6 months and can be renewed.

4. Why was chlorpromazine, introduced in the 1950s, important for deinstitutionalisation?

  1. It controlled symptoms so many patients could live outside hospital
  2. It made ECT unnecessary for all patients
  3. It permanently cured schizophrenia
  4. It removed the need for any community support
Show answer

A: It controlled symptoms so many patients could live outside hospital. Antipsychotics reduced symptoms such as hallucinations and agitation, making discharge feasible. They do not cure the disorder or remove the need for support.

5. Goffman (1961) described asylums as 'total institutions'. This means they:

  1. were funded wholly by central government
  2. controlled all aspects of residents' lives, cut off from wider society
  3. gave every patient the same drug
  4. admitted only voluntary patients
Show answer

B: controlled all aspects of residents' lives, cut off from wider society. A total institution has residents living, working and sleeping together under one authority, cut off from society. This leads to loss of identity and institutionalisation.

6. A patient is discharged, stops attending the community clinic, relapses and is readmitted three times in a year. This pattern is known as:

  1. institutionalisation
  2. moral treatment
  3. the revolving door
  4. trans-institutionalisation
Show answer

C: the revolving door. The revolving door describes repeated cycles of discharge, relapse and readmission, usually caused by poor community support or non-adherence to treatment.

7. Leff et al.'s TAPS study followed long-stay patients moved from Friern and Claybury hospitals. What did it mainly find?

  1. Most were readmitted within a month
  2. Most became homeless
  3. Symptoms worsened sharply for nearly all
  4. Most coped well and had improved social networks
Show answer

D: Most coped well and had improved social networks. TAPS reported generally positive outcomes: symptoms stable or improved, wider friendships and satisfaction. This supports community care, but it covered discharged long-stay patients only.

8. Which is the best example of trans-institutionalisation?

  1. A patient moved between wards in one hospital
  2. A patient with schizophrenia, lacking community support, ending up in prison
  3. A patient treated at home by a CPN
  4. A patient discharged to live with family
Show answer

B: A patient with schizophrenia, lacking community support, ending up in prison. Trans-institutionalisation is when people leave psychiatric hospitals but end up in other institutions, such as prisons or nursing homes, because community provision is inadequate.

9. Which statement about care in the community in England is correct?

  1. It began only after the 2007 amendment
  2. It required all patients to be detained for 6 months
  3. It was introduced by the Lunacy Act 1845
  4. 1990 Act made local authorities assess needs and arrange care
Show answer

D: 1990 Act made local authorities assess needs and arrange care. The NHS and Community Care Act 1990 gave local authorities responsibility for assessing need and arranging care packages. The Lunacy Act 1845 was about building asylums.

10. A hospital had 1,200 psychiatric beds in 1985 and 300 in 2005. By what percentage did bed numbers fall?

  1. 80%
  2. 75%
  3. 50%
  4. 25%
Show answer

B: 75%. (1200 − 300) ÷ 1200 = 900 ÷ 1200 = 0.75, a 75% fall. The 25% figure is the proportion of beds remaining, not the fall.
